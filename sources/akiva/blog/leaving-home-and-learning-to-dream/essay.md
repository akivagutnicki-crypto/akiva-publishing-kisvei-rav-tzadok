## 1. The Night Before You Leave

Most of us remember the night before we left home.

The duffel bag by the door. The suitcase that would not close. A mother who kept finding one more thing to pack, and a father who suddenly had a great deal to say, or nothing at all. Maybe it was the night before yeshiva or seminary in Eretz Yisrael. Maybe it was the night before a new job in a new city, the night before a wedding, or the night after the job ended and you did not yet know what came next. Some people leave home with a blessing in their ears. Others leave with the door slamming behind them. Most of us, if we are honest, leave with a little of both.

Yaakov left with both.

״וַיֵּצֵא יַעֲקֹב מִבְּאֵר שָׁבַע וַיֵּלֶךְ חָרָנָה״, “Yaakov departed from Be’er Sheva and went to Charan.”[^1] Every commentator stops at the doubled language. If the Torah tells us he went to Charan, why tell us that he left Be’er Sheva?

Rav Tzadok HaKohen of Lublin answers in Kometz HaMinchah that the two verbs belong to two different parents. *Vayeitzei*, he departed, was Rivkah’s command. It was an escape, a flight from a brother who had sworn to kill him, and nothing more; she planned to send for him once Esav’s anger cooled. *Vayelech*, he went, was Yitzchak’s command: go to Charan, find your wife, and build your home. Rivkah never told Yitzchak about the danger. One parent sent him away to save his life. The other sent him toward his future. Only Hashem saw the whole story.

::: interlinear
וּשְׁנֵיהֶם הָיוּ מְכֻוָּן אֶחָד מַמָּשׁ, — **Yet the two were truly a single purpose.** — וְזֶה שֶׁאָמְרוּ זִכְרוֹנָם לִבְרָכָה: ״בֶּן יוֹמוֹ״, — **This is the meaning of our Sages’ statement: “On that very day.”** — רָצָה לוֹמַר, שֶׁהַיּוֹם גּוֹרֵם וּמְיֻחָד לְכָל מַעֲשֶׂה, — **That is, the day contributes to each deed and is specially appointed for it.** — וְכָאן ״בֶּן יוֹמוֹ״, שֶׁהַכֹּל מְכֻוָּן אֶחָד. — **Here, “on that very day” means that everything serves one purpose.**

::: cite
Kometz HaMinchah II:70
:::
:::

The flight and the mission were one journey. They only looked like two because nobody could see all of it at once.

Think of the person who was let go on a Tuesday afternoon and, on the drive home, took a call that led to the work they were meant to do. Think of the couple whose apartment fell through, who moved to the only place they could find, and who met the neighbors who became family. In the moment, the day is a crisis. Years later, it is the hinge the whole story turned on. Rav Tzadok calls this *ben yomo*, the day itself “causes” and is appointed for what happens in it. What we ran from and what we were sent toward were stitched together on purpose.

There is another detail that makes this leaving harder. Yaakov had just received his father’s berachos, the high point of his young life. Rav Tzadok applies to this moment the saying *ein chozek kechassidus bitechilasah*, there is no fervor like the fervor of the beginning. And then, immediately, *vayeitzei*. According to the Midrash quoted by Rashi, Elifaz overtook him on the road and took everything he had. Yaakov arrives in Charan with nothing.[^2]

Anyone who has come home from an inspiring Shabbaton, a first trip to Israel or a year in yeshiva knows this feeling. The lights are on, the songs are still in your head, and then Sunday morning comes. The real journey usually begins the day after the inspiration ends.

## 2. The Helper Who Stands Against You

Where does Hashem send Yaakov, the *ish tam*, the man of whom it is said *lo nachash beYaakov*, there is no sorcery in Jacob? To Aram, whose letters spell *ramai*, a swindler, and to Bnei Kedem, a people steeped in divination. He spends twenty years working for Lavan, the most accomplished liar in the Torah.

Why there? Why not a quiet town full of honest people who would appreciate an honest man?

Rav Tzadok’s answer reaches back to the first marriage in the Torah, *ezer kenegdo*, a helper opposite him.[^3] He reads the words literally. The help that completes a person comes from what stands against him:

::: interlinear
וְהַיְנוּ, הָעֵזֶר לִשְׁלֵמוּת הוּא מִצַּד הַנֶּגְדִּי לוֹ; — **That is, help toward perfection comes from what is contrary to him.** — כַּאֲשֶׁר הוּא גַּם כֵּן בִּשְׁלֵמוּת, — **When he is complete in that respect as well,** — אָז גַּם כֵּן מְבֹרָר בִּשְׁלֵמוּת, — **his perfection is then also established.**

::: cite
Kometz HaMinchah II:70
:::
:::

Avraham, whose nature was boundless kindness, was tested ten times with trials that ran against that nature, including the command to bind his own son. Yaakov, the man of truth, was tested by deception at every turn: the lentil stew, the berachos, the switched bride, the speckled sheep, and finally his own sons’ silence about Yosef. Truth that has never met a lie is untested innocence. Truth that has survived Lavan is strength.

We know this from life. The impatient parent is given the child who needs endless patience. The man who wants to be liked is given the job where he has to say no. The perfectionist is given a home that will never quite be in order. It is tempting to read these as cosmic mistakes. Rav Tzadok reads them as an address. Your hardest situation is often aimed precisely at the part of you that is not yet whole.

He takes this one step further, and it is a bold step. “So too Israel: their sins are the main means by which their perfection is clarified.” The word *chet* means a lack, a *chisaron*, the way an archer “misses” the mark. A failure shows you exactly where the missing piece is. That does not make failure good. It makes it useful. The place you keep falling is the place your teshuvah is waiting for you.

And when the distance between who we are and who we need to become looks impossible, Rav Tzadok quotes the Midrash about the fool who says, “When will I ever learn all the Torah in the wise man’s heart?” He does not tell us the fool is wrong about the distance. *Arukah me’eretz midah*, its measure really is longer than the earth.[^4] That is why Yaakov was given *kefitzas haderech*, a road that folded up beneath him. Beyond the partner who stands against us, there is a third partner in every struggle: *ilmalei HaKadosh Baruch Hu ozro*, without the Holy One’s help, no one could prevail.[^5]

Here is a story that shows how far one person can go with that help. Rabbi Akiva was forty years old and had never learned. One day he stood at a well and noticed a stone with a hollow worn into it. He asked who had carved it. He was told: the water, drop by drop, day after day. He reasoned that if soft water could carve hard stone, the words of Torah could surely carve a heart of flesh. With his wife Rachel’s encouragement, he left home to learn, and he was gone for twenty-four years.[^6]

A man at a well, a stone and a long road away from home. Rabbi Akiva’s story begins where Yaakov’s does. Yaakov, too, arrives at a well in Charan, finds a stone too heavy for the shepherds, and rolls it off by himself when he sees Rachel. The distance never shrinks by wishing. It folds, one drop and one day at a time, with Heaven’s help.

## 3. Finding Yourself Far from Home

Rav Tzadok asks a question that sounds historical but is really personal. Yaakov was to father the twelve tribes. Why were eleven of them born in exile, in Lavan’s house, and not in Eretz Yisrael?

Because, he explains, in the Land, the world of Yitzchak who never left it, there is *hakarah berurah*, clear awareness of Hashem. Where everything is clear, *ein sham yegiah va’avodah*, there is no toil and no freely chosen service. A complete nation cannot be built in effortless daylight. It is built *be’es harichuk*, in a time of distance, when you have to decide who you are without anyone watching.

This is why the first year away from home can be the most important year of a young person’s life. At home, davening is what everyone does at seven-thirty. In a dorm room, in a new city, in an office where nobody keeps Shabbos, it becomes a choice. The same is true of every adult who has moved somewhere unfamiliar. You find out what you believe when your surroundings stop believing it for you.

Natan Sharansky spent nine years in Soviet prisons, many months of them in punishment cells. In his memoir he describes how a small book of Tehillim from his wife Avital stayed with him through those years. When he was finally released in 1986 and told to walk straight across the bridge to freedom, he walked in a zigzag, a last refusal to let his jailers decide even the shape of his steps.[^7] He had found the most free part of himself in the least free place on earth. Yaakov, says Rav Tzadok, was called *mitah sheleimah*, the bed whose children were all whole, not because he stayed sheltered but because *gam berichuk makom lo zaz midibuko*, even in a distant place he never let go of his attachment to Hashem. From that journey comes the Gemara’s promise: wherever Israel was exiled, the Shechinah went with them.[^8]

On the way, Yaakov stops for the night and *vayikach me’avnei hamakom*, he takes stones from the place. Rav Tzadok notes that Avraham called this holy site a mountain and Yitzchak called it a field. Both are open spaces anyone can wander into. Only Yaakov called it a *bayis*, Beis Elokim, a house: a bounded, private, protected space.[^9] He put the stones around his head, because every action begins in the mind. Guarding what you let into your head is how you lay the first stones of a home.

By morning, the stones had fused into one. The Midrash says Yaakov understood from this that there was no *pesoles*, no waste, left in him. A house is built when separate stones become one structure. A self is built the same way: the scattered pieces of a life, past, present and future, inner and outer, joined into one. And then Rav Tzadok writes the line that, for me, is the heart of this whole parashah:

::: interlinear
כִּי הַשְּׁכִינָה נִקְרֵאת ״אֲנִי״, — **For the Divine Presence is called “I”:** — רָצָה לוֹמַר: שֶׁהָאָדָם מְדַבֵּר ״אֲנִי״ וְרוֹצֶה לוֹמַר הַשֵּׁם יִתְבָּרַךְ, — **meaning that when a person says “I,” he means Hashem, blessed be He,** — שֶׁהַשֵּׁם יִתְבָּרַךְ שׁוֹכֵן בְּקִרְבּוֹ מַמָּשׁ — **for Hashem dwells literally within him,** — ״בְּקִרְבְּךָ קָדוֹשׁ״, וּשְׁכִינָה מְדַבֶּרֶת מִגְּרוֹנוֹ. — **“the Holy One in your midst,” and the Divine Presence speaks through his throat.**

::: cite
Kometz HaMinchah II:71
:::
:::

We spend a lot of energy trying to “find ourselves.” Rav Tzadok says that when you finally find your deepest “I,” you find that Someone else is already living there.

That inner truth passes to children even when nothing is said. After the Holocaust, a rabbi went searching for Jewish children who had been hidden in monasteries and with Christian families. Many had been taken in so young that they did not know they were Jewish, and those caring for them would not say. So he stood among them and called out *Shema Yisrael*. Some of the children began to cry, and called out for their mother and father.[^10] Years of distance had not erased what a parent had planted. The stones had already been laid around their heads.

## 4. Learning to Dream

״וַיִּשְׁכַּב בַּמָּקוֹם הַהוּא, וַיַּחֲלֹם, וְהִנֵּה סֻלָּם מֻצָּב אַרְצָה״, “He lay down in that place, and he dreamed, and behold, a ladder was set on the ground.”

We usually think of dreaming as what happens when we stop paying attention. Rav Tzadok reads it the other way around. Dreaming is Yaakov’s level. He has reached a place “where further toil and struggle are no longer needed, and even lying down is good. This is the house, where there is rest, whereas the mountain and the field require labor.” This, he says, is the level of Shabbos, the *sheva* of Be’er Sheva that Yaakov carried with him when he left.

Anyone who has learned a skill deeply knows a little of this. The musician who has practiced for years hears the piece in her sleep. The new driver grips the wheel, and the experienced one drives home thinking of other things. When the waking life is aligned, the inner life follows. Rav Tzadok describes a person whose imaginings and the unguarded thoughts of his heart all see one thing: *vehinei Hashem*, and behold, Hashem. The goal of a Jewish life is not to escape into dreams. It is to become so whole that even your dreams are holy.

Then comes the ladder. The Midrash says *sulam zeh Sinai*: the ladder is Sinai, with every Jew standing somewhere on it, from the simplest person at the bottom to Moshe Rabbeinu at the top.[^11] Nobody starts at the top, and nobody is left off.

The angels on the ladder go up and then down, *olim veyordim*. Rav Tzadok explains why: a greater ascent is impossible without a descent first. You reach a level, and then you fall. Every person who has tried to grow knows this rhythm. The diet lasts three weeks. The new seder in Gemara lasts until Pesach. The resolution to speak more gently lasts until the kids are late for the bus. It is easy to conclude that the climb was fake.

Look at the next words: *vehinei Hashem nitzav alav*, and behold, Hashem was standing over him. They come right after the descent. Hashem does not leave when you fall. Finding Him there, at the bottom of the rung, is what lifts you higher than you were before.

Why was Sinai, of all mountains, chosen for the Torah? Because it was the lowest.[^12] Hashem says *ani es daka*, I dwell with the crushed and the humble. Rav Tzadok connects this to *Hashem tzilecha*, Hashem is your shadow: as a person lowers himself, Hashem, as it were, lowers Himself to meet him. That is when, the Midrash says, the whole land folded up beneath Yaakov. When you stop pretending to be a mountain, the distance closes.

## 5. The Promise of Return

At the end of the dream, Hashem makes Yaakov promises: the land, the children, and *vehinei anochi imach*, behold, I am with you. Rav Tzadok hears in *anochi* the first word of the Ten Commandments. If a person keeps *I am with you* before his eyes, the whole Torah follows from it. That is why the Shulchan Aruch opens with *shivisi Hashem lenegdi samid*, I have set Hashem before me always.

And then the final promise: *vahashivosicha... ki lo e’ezavcha*, I will bring you back, for I will not leave you. Rav Tzadok reads this as teshuvah, the level of King David and Mashiach ben David, who completes the chariot of the Avos.[^13]

This is the promise for everyone who has left home, whether they walked out with a blessing, ran out with a brother at their heels, or simply drifted. You may have left the Torah of your parents, or a version of yourself you liked better, or a sense that Hashem was close. The promise does not say you will never go far. It says *I will bring you back*. Every departure carries the seed of its return.

So if tonight you find yourself in a strange place with a stone under your head, remember what Yaakov discovered when he woke up. *Achen yesh Hashem bamakom hazeh, ve’anochi lo yadati*. Hashem was in this place, and I did not know it. The ladder was standing exactly where he lay down. It still is.
